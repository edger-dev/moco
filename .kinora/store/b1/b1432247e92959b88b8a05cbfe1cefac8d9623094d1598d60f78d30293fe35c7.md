# Spec — a job is a process group, and stopping one stops the tree

**Contract.** Every job leads its **own process group**. Stopping a job signals
that group, not the single process the supervisor spawned: `SIGTERM` first, then
`SIGKILL` once a grace period has passed without the job exiting. The escalation
rides the supervision tick the daemon already runs.

**Why the group.** A job is very often a shell wrapping the real program —
`sh -c "server --port @MOCO_PORT"` is the sanctioned way to read an injected
port. Signalling only the process we spawned kills the shell and orphans the
server, so the job reads as **stopped while still holding its port**, and the
next start fails on a conflict that names nothing useful. Anything short of the
group makes "stop" a statement about bookkeeping rather than about processes.

**Why `TERM` before `KILL`.** A service asked to stop has real work to do —
flush a buffer, remove a socket, finish a request — and whether it is allowed to
is the difference between a clean restart and a corrupted state file. Five
seconds is long enough for that and short enough that a wedged job is not
mistaken for a working one.

**Why escalate at all.** The polite signal is a request, and a request can be
declined: by a job that traps it, or one already too wedged to act. Without
escalation "stop" would be advisory, which is not what anyone means by it. A job
that survives even `SIGKILL` — an uninterruptible wait — is signalled once and
not re-signalled every tick forever; there is nothing further to try, and
retrying would only bury it in noise.

**The liveness guard is load-bearing.** Signalling a stale pid is a mistake;
signalling a stale process *group* is a wide one, because a reused pid may by
then have children of its own. So the recorded `(pid, start-time)` pair is
checked before any signal, exactly as every other probe does, and a pid that is
not this job is left alone. A job already in a terminal state is never
signalled.

**A missing group falls back to the pid.** A child that outlived its group
leader is still this job's child, and declining to signal it because the group
is empty would leave it running.

**This makes an adopted job killable.** Stopping used to go through the child
handle, which an adopted job does not have — so the signal reached nothing at
all while the call still returned success. A caller that asked to stop something
and got a quiet `Ok` would believe it had, which is precisely what the
foreign-workspace refusal exists to prevent one line earlier. An adopted job we
asked to stop and which is then gone reports `Killed`, not `OutcomeUnknown`:
that variant is for an end we did not cause.

**No `unsafe`.** `CommandExt::process_group` is safe and stable, so this needs no
`pre_exec` and the crate keeps its `unsafe_code = "deny"` standard.

**Rejected — `setsid` instead of `setpgid`.** It would also create a session and
detach the controlling terminal, which is a separate decision about the terminal
lens and not one this change is entitled to make.

**Rejected — killing the process tree by walking `/proc`.** Racy by
construction: a process can fork between the read and the signal, and the walk
has no way to be atomic. The kernel already maintains exactly this grouping.

**Rejected — a blocking grace period inside `kill`.** It would hold the caller,
and the registry lock, for seconds. The tick already exists and already owns
timing for restart policy and sampling.

**Escape hatch.** The grace period is configurable per registry, so a deployment
that needs a longer clean shutdown can have one.
